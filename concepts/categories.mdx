A well-organized knowledge base makes it easy for readers to find what they need — whether they're browsing or searching. Your knowledge base gives you two complementary tools for organizing content: **categories**, which define a fixed hierarchy your readers can navigate, and **tags**, which add flexible, cross-cutting labels that connect related articles regardless of where they live in the hierarchy. Used together, they create a structure that's both easy to browse and easy to filter.

## Categories

Categories form the backbone of your knowledge base's navigation. They appear in the sidebar, in breadcrumbs, and in the table of contents, giving readers a clear map of how your content is organized.

### Hierarchical Structure

Categories are hierarchical — you can nest child categories inside parent categories to create structured sections. For example, a top-level "Getting Started" category might contain child categories for "Account Setup," "Inviting Your Team," and "First Steps."

When a reader browses your knowledge base, they navigate through the category tree to drill down into the content they need. The sidebar reflects this hierarchy visually, with parent categories expanding to reveal their children.

### Parent and Child Categories

- **Parent categories** act as broad topic areas or sections. They can contain both articles and child categories.
- **Child categories** are nested under a parent and represent more specific subtopics. They can themselves have children, though keeping nesting shallow (2–3 levels) makes navigation easier for readers.

Every article must belong to at least one category. When you create an article, you assign it to the most specific category that fits — not to a broad parent category if a more precise child category exists.

### How Categories Appear in Navigation

Categories render in the left-hand sidebar in the order you define. You can reorder categories and articles by dragging them in the content manager. The breadcrumb trail at the top of each article page shows the full path from the root to the article's category, helping readers orient themselves.

<Tip>
  Plan your category structure before you start adding large amounts of content. It's much easier to build a clear hierarchy upfront than to reorganize dozens of articles after the fact. Sketch out your top-level sections and one level of subcategories before you begin publishing.
</Tip>

## Tags

Tags are free-form labels you attach to individual articles. Unlike categories — which place an article in exactly one location in the hierarchy — tags let you group related articles that span multiple categories.

### What Tags Are For

Use tags to express dimensions of your content that don't fit neatly into the category hierarchy. For example:

- Tag articles by product area (`billing`, `integrations`, `security`) even when those articles live in different how-to and reference categories
- Tag articles by audience (`admins`, `developers`, `end-users`) so readers can filter for content relevant to their role
- Tag articles by content type (`tutorial`, `troubleshooting`, `release-note`) to help readers find the format they prefer

### Filtering by Tag

Readers can click any tag displayed on an article to see all other articles sharing that tag, regardless of which category they belong to. This makes tags a powerful discovery tool — a reader troubleshooting a billing issue can click the `billing` tag to surface every billing-related article across all sections of your knowledge base instantly.

Tags also appear as filter options on the search results page, letting readers narrow results by one or more tags after performing a keyword search.

## Best Practices

Following a few simple conventions when you set up your categories and tags will save you significant reorganization work later.

<CardGroup cols={1}>
  <Card title="Keep category depth to 2–3 levels" icon="layer-group">
    Deeply nested categories become difficult to navigate and hard to maintain. If you find yourself creating a fourth or fifth level of nesting, consider whether a flat structure with tags would serve readers better.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Use broad categories with specific tags" icon="sliders">
    Broad categories like "API Reference" or "Troubleshooting" work well as stable navigation anchors. Specific, precise tags handle the nuances — tagging articles with `webhooks`, `authentication`, or `rate-limits` within the API Reference section, for example.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Avoid duplicating content across categories" icon="copy">
    If an article seems to belong in two categories, it's usually better to place it in the most relevant one and use tags to surface it in other contexts. Duplicate articles create maintenance problems: when the information changes, you have to update multiple copies.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>
