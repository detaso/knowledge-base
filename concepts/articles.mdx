Articles are the fundamental building blocks of your knowledge base. Each article is a self-contained piece of content — a guide, a reference document, a FAQ entry, or an announcement — that you create, organize, and publish for your readers. Understanding how articles are structured and how they move through their lifecycle helps you build a knowledge base that stays accurate, well-organized, and easy to maintain.

## Article Anatomy

Every article in your knowledge base is made up of several key fields that control how it appears, where it lives, and who can find it.

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Title" icon="heading">
    The article's name, shown in navigation, search results, and at the top of the page. Write titles that clearly describe the content — readers use them to decide whether to click.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Body Content" icon="align-left">
    The main substance of the article, written using the rich text editor or Markdown. This is the content that gets indexed for search and read by your audience.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Category" icon="folder">
    The hierarchical location of the article within your knowledge base. Every article belongs to at least one category, which determines where it appears in the sidebar and navigation.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Tags" icon="tag">
    Flexible labels you attach to an article to link it with related content across different categories. Tags help readers discover related articles through filtering.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Author" icon="user">
    The workspace member who owns the article. The author is displayed to readers and receives notifications about comments or suggested edits.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Publish Status" icon="circle-dot">
    The current stage of the article in its lifecycle: Draft, In Review, Published, or Archived. Publish status controls who can see the article and whether it appears in search results.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

The **last updated date** is recorded automatically every time an article is saved. It appears on the published article so readers can judge how current the information is — keep this in mind when reviewing older content.

## Article Lifecycle

Articles move through four distinct states from creation to retirement. Understanding this workflow helps your team collaborate on content without accidentally publishing unfinished work.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Draft">
    When you create a new article, it starts as a Draft. Draft articles are only visible to workspace members with editor access — they never appear in public search results or navigation. Use the draft stage to write, research, and iterate freely.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Review">
    When the article is ready for feedback, move it to Review. This signals to teammates and approvers that the content is complete and awaiting sign-off. Reviewers can leave comments and request changes without publishing the article prematurely.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Published">
    Once approved, publish the article to make it live. Published articles appear in your knowledge base navigation, show up in search results, and are accessible according to their visibility settings (public or workspace-only).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Archived">
    When content is outdated but you want to preserve it for reference, archive the article. Archived articles are removed from navigation and search results but remain accessible to editors via a direct link. You can restore an archived article to published at any time.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Note>
  Draft articles are only visible to workspace members who have Editor access or higher. Readers and external visitors cannot see drafts, even if they have the direct URL. Always keep sensitive work-in-progress content in draft state until it's ready to publish.
</Note>

## Article Types

Not every piece of content serves the same purpose. Choosing the right article type helps you write more focused content and helps readers know what to expect before they start reading.

<Accordion title="How-to Guides">
  How-to guides walk readers through completing a specific task, step by step. Use this type for procedural content like "Setting up SSO" or "Inviting team members." Structure them with numbered steps and clear success criteria so readers know when they're done.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Reference Docs">
  Reference docs provide structured, factual information that readers look up rather than read cover-to-cover — API parameters, configuration options, keyboard shortcuts, and similar material. Prioritize scannability: use tables, definition lists, and short descriptions over long paragraphs.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="FAQs">
  FAQ articles answer a single common question directly and concisely. They work well as standalone articles grouped in a "Frequently Asked Questions" category, or as supplementary articles linked from longer how-to guides. Keep each FAQ article focused on one question so search can surface it precisely.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Announcements">
  Announcements communicate changes, new features, or important updates to your readers. Unlike evergreen content, announcements are time-sensitive — include the date prominently and consider archiving old announcements once they're no longer relevant to keep your knowledge base uncluttered.
</Accordion>
